## 1. The task

Your group has been assigned one article. **You are not going to explain it.**

You are going to use it to make a case.

> **[FIN VERSION]**
> That article is what landed on your desk this morning. We are your investment committee. You have three minutes.
> What do we do about it, and why?

> **[AFM VERSION]**
> That article describes something happening in your sector. You are the finance and control team. You have three minutes in front of the board.
> What does this mean for us, and what do you recommend?

The article is a **starting point, not a thesis to defend.** If you agree with it, say so and go further. If you disagree, say that instead. Your position is yours.

**Two groups share each article.** You will see what the other group did with the same source, and you will give each other feedback. That contrast is part of the lesson.

## 2. What you deliver

| | |
|---|---|
| **Pitch** | 3 minutes. Hard stop. |
| **Presenter** | One person. Not two. |
| **Slides** | Maximum **6**. PowerPoint or PDF. No audio, no video. |
| **Upload** | To Blackboard by **Saturday 13.00**. After that the content is frozen. |
| **Written draft** | Plain text, submitted **Saturday 10.15**. See section 4. |

### Two things your pitch may not do

**Do not introduce yourselves.** No "hello, we are group 7."

**Do not summarise the article.** We have all read it.

This is not a style preference. Investors spend an average of **3 minutes 44 seconds** on an entire pitch deck, and the first slide gets more than twice the attention of any other. Your opening is the only part of your pitch that someone is guaranteed to hear. Do not spend it on logistics.

Open with your idea, or with the conflict.

---

## 3. Saturday afternoon: how it works

Every one of you is an investor. After each pitch:

**You fund it, or you don't.** You have **three tickets of €50,000**. Spend them when you see something that convinces you. When they are gone, they are gone — and you cannot undo a "no". At least one ticket must be spent in the first half of the session and one in the second.

**You give written feedback on every pitch**, including the ones you don't fund: one thing that worked, one thing you would improve. Short. Specific. I see who wrote what; **groups receive all feedback anonymised.** So write what you actually think.

**Phones face down during the three minutes.** You get a 30-second window to submit after each pitch. While someone is presenting, look at them — you will be up there shortly.

**One of you will be drawn at random to ask a question out loud.** Your question must refer to something the group actually said. A question that could have been asked of any group does not count.

**And the group's answer will be given by a member chosen at random by me — not the presenter.** Everyone in your group needs to know the argument well enough to defend it.

"I don't know, let me check and come back to you" is a legitimate professional answer. Not knowing something is not a failure.

At the end, we look at **how much capital each group raised.**

## 4. The written draft — Saturday 10.15

Before anyone opens PowerPoint, your group submits a plain text document. No design, no formatting, no bullet-point decoration.

1. **Your core idea, in one sentence.** If it does not fit in one sentence, you do not have it yet.
2. **The conflict.** What is at stake? Who does it hurt?
3. **Your recommendation.**
4. **Your three supporting arguments.**
5. **Who contributed what.** Names.

Why we do this: writing the argument before designing the slides is what separates a group that has a thesis from a group that has slides. Amazon runs its meetings on six-page written memos for the same reason.

## 5. Use of AI

The syllabus and the honour code state that AI may not be used to produce your presentation. Here is what that means in practice this weekend, phase by phase.

**Friday 17.15 to Saturday 13.00 — no AI on the substance.**

Your idea, your conflict, your argument, your structure and your slides are yours. The written draft is the evidence of that.

You may use your laptop and AI freely **to find and verify sources**: other articles, data, charts, background. That is research, and it is expected — the article is only the beginning.

What AI cannot do for you is decide what you think. That is the entire point of the exercise.

**Saturday from 13.00 — content frozen, AI opens as a rehearsal coach.**

Once your file is uploaded, nothing in it changes. From that moment you may use AI as a rehearsal partner: deliver your pitch out loud to it and ask for feedback against the evaluation grid below. Do it five times. Record yourself on your phone and watch it back.

This is the part of your preparation that almost nobody does, and it is the part that decides the outcome.

---

## 6. What you are evaluated on

Not graded — this seminar is pass/fail. But this is the grid, and it is what your peers will be judging when they decide where to put their money.

1. **Clarity of the core idea** — is there one, and only one?
2. **Balance of emotion and fact** — does it give us a reason to care, not just a reason to believe?
3. **Structure and flow**
4. **Language and resources**
5. **Delivery**

A note on the second one, because it is where this class consistently loses. You will produce excellent *logos*: data, mechanism, evidence. You will do it without thinking, because it is your native language. Stating facts does not tell anyone **why those facts matter to them.** That is what the other appeals are for.

Seventeen groups can explain an article well. The one that wins will be the one that makes us feel something about it.
